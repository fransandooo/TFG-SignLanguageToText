\chapter*{Abstract}

Sign language recognition serves to mitigate communication barriers between deaf or hard-of-hearing
signers and non-signing people. This Final Degree Project develops and evaluates a research
prototype for word-level American Sign Language (ASL) recognition from short video clips. The work
first reviews the accessibility problem, the computer vision methods used for recognition and the
technical requirements of the system. It then follows the methodological evolution of the project,
from initial approaches based on hand detection and static letter recognition towards a
sequence-based model that represents the movement of signs across frames. The final system uses
MediaPipe Hands to extract 21 3D hand landmarks per frame, normalises the resulting sequences and
classifies each clip with a bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) with a temporal attention
mechanism. The experiments evaluate architecture choices, sequence length, cross-dataset evaluation
and vocabulary size using the Word-Level American Sign Language (WLASL) and Microsoft American Sign
Language (MS-ASL) datasets. The results show consistent performance for reduced vocabularies and
across datasets, but accuracy decreases as the number of classes increases. The landmark
representation also abstracts away identifying visual information, offering a privacy-conscious
alternative to raw video, although the prototype remains a research system rather than a
production-ready translator.
